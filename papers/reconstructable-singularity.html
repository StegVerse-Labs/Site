Reconstructable Singularity

A Governed Framework for Continuity Reconstruction, Reachability, and Minimum Perspective Resolution

Research formalism v0.1 Minimum continuity-resolving perspective set Affirmative + exclusionary evidence Empirical validation not established
Research boundary. This paper presents the Reconstructable Singularity as a separate mathematical and governance research formalism. It does not supersede the existing The Reconstructive Singularity scholarly review. Publication does not establish empirical proof, physical collapse or access to histories, universal completeness, execution authority, custody authority, admissibility authority, certification, or release authority.

Abstract

The Reconstructable Singularity is the point at which a connected set of observational frameworks contains enough affirmative and exclusionary evidence to reduce the admissibility-consistent continuity-history space to exactly one surviving reconstruction. The singularity is therefore epistemic and reconstructive rather than a physical collapse of many histories into one.

This updated formulation preserves the broader research questions that motivated the earlier Reconstructive Singularity paper—reality reachability, temporal reconstruction, cross-domain correspondence, identity continuity, uncertainty, common causes, residuals, and the separation of knowledge from authority—while replacing its central threshold with an explicit minimum-perspective formalism. The threshold can be written as singleton reconstruction, injectivity, or an equivalent minimum hitting-set problem over observers capable of distinguishing candidate histories.

1. Continuity model

Let a realized trajectory be a sequence of states, cost-bearing transitions, and resulting states. For adjacent realized states, one realized transition connects the prior state to the resulting state. Evaluation, ALLOW, DENY, ESCALATE, and inadmissible execution can all be represented as state changes.

S_j --(T_j, C_j)--> S_(j+1)

Continuity preserves attribution among prior state, transition, incurred cost, and resulting state. It does not certify that the transition was admissible.

2. Candidate-history space

Let Γ be the histories representable within the observational framework, and Γ_A the subset consistent with the complete admissibility matrix: required, permitted, forbidden, and excluded relations; authority and policy conditions; transition-cost constraints; and continuity constraints.

For observer subset I, let each observer contribute positive evidence O_i+ and exclusionary evidence O_i-. The surviving continuity histories are:

C_A(I) = Γ_A ∩ ⋂(i∈I) O_i+ ∩ ⋂(i∈I) ¬O_i-

3. Reconstructable singularity

A reconstructable singularity is reached when the combined evidence leaves exactly one admissibility-consistent continuity history:

|C_A(I)| = 1

The multiplicity that disappears is the unresolved reconstruction space. The formalism does not claim that several realized physical histories existed and then collapsed.

4. Minimum perspective threshold

The minimum number of perspectives needed to resolve the history is:

k_A* = min { |I| : |C_A(I)| = 1 }

The threshold is structural, not merely numerical. Many observers can repeat the same information and add no resolving power, while one differently situated observer can exclude an entire unresolved region.

5. Injectivity and hitting-set formulation

Let each observer have a trajectory-observation map g_i and let G_I be their combined map. The selected perspective set is sufficient when G_I is injective over Γ_A:

k_Γ* = min { |I| : G_I is injective on Γ_A }

For each distinct pair of candidate histories γ and γ′, define D_(γ,γ′) as the observers capable of distinguishing them. A resolving set must intersect every such D set. This produces an equivalent minimum hitting-set formulation and connects the model to observability, identifiability, and resolving-set theory.

6. Exclusionary evidence is first-class

Observers do not need to agree in order to contribute useful reconstruction evidence. Their relationship may be affirmatively complementary, exclusionarily complementary, resolution-different, coordinate-different, or genuinely contradictory.

Key distinction: disagreement is not automatically a defect. An observation that establishes what cannot occupy a region of the admissibility matrix can be exactly what resolves an otherwise ambiguous history.

7. Reachability and cross-domain relations

The earlier Reconstructive Singularity paper treated reality reachability and cross-domain transformation as central problems. Those concepts remain useful here, but they now sit below the reconstruction threshold rather than defining it.

A state or domain can be classified as known reachable, reachable but unresolved, counterfactual reachable, currently inaccessible but relationally established, relationally unestablished, contradicted, or indeterminate. None of these classifications alone establishes a unique continuity history.

Likewise, cross-domain correspondence requires a declared transformation relation and held-out validation. Contact, relational extent, fitted transformation, and validated transformation are distinct evidentiary stages. Correspondence does not by itself prove continuity or identity.

8. Identity continuity, temporal drift, and common causes

A reconstructed relation must preserve whatever identity invariants are appropriate to the domain: cryptographic continuity, causal ancestry, conserved quantities, structural invariants, receipt-chain continuity, or another declared criterion.

correspondence ≠ identity transformation fit ≠ continuity continuity ≠ authority

Transformations may drift over time, so a mapping inferred at one time cannot silently be treated as stable. Apparent cross-domain agreement may also arise from common causes, duplicated data, synchronized intervention, shared clocks, copied identifiers, selection bias, or observer coupling. Independent resolving power requires genuinely distinct information.

9. Uncertainty, unknown unknowns, and residuals

The formalism does not require stochastic state transitions. Epistemic uncertainty can remain even in a deterministic system because an observer may lack intermediate states, relevant variables, or distinguishing evidence.

observed_result = modeled contribution + known unknown contribution + unknown unknown contribution + transition waste / information loss + unexplained residual

Unknown unknowns cannot be parameterized in advance, but their possibility must remain visible through anomaly channels, residual structure, uncertainty bounds, and explicit model-incompleteness indicators.

10. State observability is weaker than trajectory reconstruction

A set of observers may uniquely identify a current state without uniquely reconstructing the path that produced it. If H_I is the combined state-observation map, state-level observability requires injectivity over admissible states. Full trajectory reconstruction requires enough retained continuity structure to distinguish admissible histories, not merely endpoints.

11. Falsification and empirical program

The framework should fail closed when multiple candidate histories remain indistinguishable, when exclusionary evidence is inconsistent with retained observations, when observer independence is falsely assumed, when identity invariants fail, or when held-out observations contradict the claimed transformation or reconstruction.

A valid empirical program therefore needs a bounded target domain, admissible observation classes, immutable provenance, preregistered falsifiers, positive and negative controls, common-cause analysis, temporal-drift tests, identity criteria, held-out validation, residual accounting, and independent replication.

machine-checkable formalism ≠ empirical proof singleton fixture ≠ universal uniqueness reconstruction ≠ authority publication ≠ release or execution

13. Relationship to Admissible Existence

Admissible Existence asks whether entities and transitions preserve standing, continuity, and recoverability under governing constraints. Reconstructable Singularity asks when the connected observational evidence is sufficient to reduce the admissibility-consistent continuity-history space to one.

A uniquely reconstructed history can still contain an inadmissible transition. Reconstruction answers what continuity history is supported; admissibility remains a separate governing determination.

Related concepts — distinct, not revisions

Reconstructable Singularity is this minimum-perspective continuity-reconstruction formalism.

The Reconstructive Singularity is a separate scholarly review focused on broader reality reconstruction, reachability, temporal reconstruction, cross-domain transformation, identity continuity, uncertainty, and admissible transition.

Reconstruction Singularity is a separate Admissibility Wiki formalism describing an institutional threshold where reconstruction demand exceeds reconstruction capacity and reconstruction becomes an operating layer of governance.

Identity rule: these three names identify related but distinct works. None is an alias, replacement, or superseding version of another.

14. Conclusion

Reconstructable Singularity defines a testable minimum-perspective criterion for continuity reconstruction. Related reachability, temporal reconstruction, cross-domain mapping, identity continuity, uncertainty, and common-cause questions remain useful context but do not make this paper a revision of the separate Reconstructive Singularity scholarly review.

The central statement is:

The reconstructable-singularity threshold is the minimum connected set of observational frameworks whose combined affirmative and exclusionary constraints reduce the admissibility-consistent continuity histories to one.
