When AI Services Fail

Computational Overcharging and the Cost of Failed Prompts

Research edition v1.0.3: 29 October 2026 · three-model adversarial review (ChatGPT, Kimi, Claude)

When an AI service fails, it does not always tell you. It can consume the prompts, the executions, the quota you paid for — deliver work that failed or never conformed — and present the failure to you as an ordinary completion. This is not speculation. In one source-verified case in this record, a failure the provider's own systems detected was surfaced to the client as a success. The charge stood. The customer carried the cost of detecting it, disputing it, and redoing the work.

You are being asked to trust these services with your work, your money, and increasingly your vote-adjacent information — while the rules for what happens when they fail are unpublished, inconsistent, or written so that the burden of their failure lands on you. Most customers never learn any of this, because there is no receipt they can inspect and no standard anyone is held to.

Read the record below and assess it yourself — the evidence is preserved so that you can. The companion paper, ChatGPT Bias and Election Integrity, follows from this one: when the question is political, the framing itself can move. Voters, start with Before You Vote.

Research scope

AI services sell finite computational entitlements: prompts, executions, tokens, quota capacity or usage windows. The paper examines what happens to those entitlements when an execution fails or delivers materially nonconforming work. It defines computational overcharging as the consumption or retention of a customer's finite computational entitlement beyond the amount justified by delivered performance under an applicable failure-accounting standard. A separate monetary charge is not required.

Findings and limits

Providers differ demonstrably in three places: what they publish about failed-execution treatment, whether a detected failure reaches the customer as a failure, and how much per-execution usage detail the customer can inspect. One provider publicly documents automatic credit protection for certain failure classes, while its other official pages describe a report-and-verify path. A source-verified case shows a detected failure surfaced to a client as an ordinary completion. Customers carry distinct burdens of detecting failure, obtaining a remedy and redoing work.

The paper does not measure any provider's backend debit or reversal. Customer-facing refund notices are not treated as verified ledger reversals, and the absence of a published rule is not treated as the absence of a mechanism. The paper makes no finding of provider intent, no provider ranking and no legal conclusion; legal questions are referred to independent counsel.

Proposed standard

Faithful failure signalling (a known failure is never presented as success), durable per-execution usage receipts, published product- and plan-specific failure rules, automatic protection of eligible entitlements for objectively verifiable provider-attributable failures, and a contestable dispute path that does not depend on criteria being declared in advance.
